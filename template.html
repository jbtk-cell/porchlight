Your air conditioner may not be protecting you.

Heat warnings describe the temperature outside. People die of the temperature inside, and almost nobody measures that. This model runs a heat-transfer simulation of every home in Connecticut's housing sample through a real heat wave — and finds that a window air conditioner leaves the rest of the home nearly as hot as having none at all.

How this works, and what it cannot tell you

The model

Each home is a two-node lumped thermal network: an air node and an envelope-mass node, integrated forward through real hourly weather. It includes conduction through walls and roof, solar gain through glazing split across orientations, sol-air temperature on the roof for top-floor units, internal gains, air exchange, and a room air conditioner that cools only the fraction of floor area it actually serves. Building characteristics — vintage, floor area, air leakage, glazing, storey, cooling equipment — come from NREL's ResStock Connecticut sample, weighted to represent the state's 1.56 million dwellings.

Why the window-opening behaviour is modelled here rather than taken from ResStock

ResStock publishes its own simulated indoor temperatures, but during this heat wave its natural ventilation is effectively switched off: measured across 53 Connecticut dwellings, windows were open a median 0.15% of timesteps during the heat wave and never once in 26 of them, while running about 10% of timesteps in an ordinary July. Taking those temperatures at face value would report a simulator artifact as a finding. So this model uses ResStock's building characteristics, which are sound, and applies its own ventilation rule: windows open when it is cooler outside than in and the interior is uncomfortable.

Limitations, stated plainly

This is a physics model, not a measurement. It has not been validated against measured indoor temperatures in Connecticut homes, and that is the single biggest thing that would strengthen it. Every dwelling is a single well-mixed zone, so it cannot represent one cool bedroom inside a hot apartment. Occupant behaviour is a simple rule, not a survey. The 32 °C line is a heat-stress reference point, not a clinical threshold, and no mortality estimate is made or implied here — converting indoor temperature into a death count requires an exposure-response relationship estimated on indoor temperature, which does not yet exist for the United States.
