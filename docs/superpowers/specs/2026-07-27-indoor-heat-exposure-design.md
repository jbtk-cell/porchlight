# Modeling indoor heat exposure to quantify avertable mortality

**Date:** 2026-07-27
**Status:** Draft for review
**Supersedes:** `2026-07-26-porchlight-design.md` (the dispatch-software version)
**Scope:** Computational only. No hardware, no partner organization, no IRB.
**Constraint:** Fast. Staged so each stage is a standalone result.

---

## 1. The scientific gap

Essentially every heat-mortality study uses the nearest weather station as the
exposure variable. The literature concedes, repeatedly and explicitly, that this
is wrong:

- *"Outdoor temperature is a poor proxy for indoor heat stress due to occupant
  behavior such as air-conditioning use and window opening."*
- *"People in temperate climates, especially elderly, typically spend about 90%
  of their time indoors, mostly at home, making indoor temperatures a more
  important exposure indicator than outdoor temperatures."*
- *"In the developed world, most fatal heat exposures occur in the indoor home
  environment, yet little is known of the correspondence between outdoor and
  indoor heat."*
- *"Indoor exposures to heat can be predicted using outdoor temperatures and
  characteristics of the housing stock to improve heat exposure assessment for
  epidemiological investigations."*

That last sentence is this project. The exposure that kills people is indoor and
unmeasured; the exposure that gets modeled is outdoor and convenient. Closing
that gap is a heat-transfer problem.

**The claim to be tested:** outdoor temperature does not merely add noise to
exposure assessment — it introduces a *systematic, structured* bias that is worst
in exactly the housing where the most vulnerable people live. If true, published
heat mortality burdens are misattributed across the housing stock, and
interventions are being targeted with a biased instrument.

## 2. Why this is tractable fast

**ResStock 2024.2 publishes hourly indoor air temperature as a timeseries
output**, for a statistically representative sample of the US residential stock,
generated by full EnergyPlus simulation. It sits in a public S3 bucket
(`oedi-data-lake`, NREL building-stock dataset) with no application and no
approval gate. Alongside every dwelling it publishes the *housing
characteristics*: vintage, geometry, insulation levels, infiltration rate, AC
presence and type.

This is a better instrument than field sensors for the question being asked,
because it provides **both the observable and the ground truth**. A reduced-order
model can be fit to the simulated indoor temperature and its recovered parameters
checked against the known envelope properties. With real sensors in real houses,
nobody knows the true R and C, so a parameter-recovery method cannot be
validated — only asserted.

ecobee's "Donate Your Data" (200,000+ homes, real measured indoor and outdoor
temperature) is the natural upgrade, but it has no documented public researcher
application path, so it is **explicitly off the critical path**. If access
arrives, it becomes the external validation set. If it does not, the limitation
is stated plainly (see section 7).

## 3. Three layers

### Layer 1 — Heat transfer (physics)

A reduced-order lumped-capacitance thermal network for a free-running dwelling.
Two model orders, so the added complexity has to earn itself:

**1R1C** — single air node:

```
C dT_in/dt = (T_out - T_in)/R + Q_sol(t) + Q_int(t)
```

with time constant `tau = RC`. Under sinusoidal forcing this has a closed-form
frequency response — amplitude ratio `1/sqrt(1 + (omega*tau)^2)` and phase lag
`arctan(omega*tau)` — which provides an exact analytical verification target for
the numerical solver.

**3R2C** — air node plus envelope thermal mass:

```
C_air dT_air/dt = (T_env - T_air)/R_in + (T_out - T_air)/R_inf + Q_int + Q_sol_dir
C_env dT_env/dt = (T_air - T_env)/R_in + (T_out - T_env)/R_out + Q_sol_abs
```

The envelope node is what produces the observed overnight behaviour: a massive
wall re-radiates stored heat inward after sunset, so the indoor minimum lags and
never reaches the outdoor minimum. This is the physics of why the top floor of an
old building stays lethal at 3 a.m.

Physical content beyond the network itself:

- **Periodic conduction.** The heat equation with sinusoidal boundary conditions
  gives amplitude damping `exp(-x/delta)` and phase lag `x/delta`, with thermal
  penetration depth `delta = sqrt(2*alpha/omega)`. This connects the lumped `tau`
  to actual wall material properties and is the analytical backbone of the whole
  model.
- **Solar gains.** Solar declination, hour angle, altitude and azimuth, incidence
  angle per facade, window SHGC and absorptivity.
- **Buoyancy-driven infiltration.** Stack effect,
  `dP = rho_0 * g * h * (T_in - T_out)/T_in`, with flow scaling as `sqrt(dP)`.
  This makes ventilation nonlinear and temperature-dependent, which matters
  because it is the dominant free cooling mechanism at night.
- **Longwave sky exchange.** Sky temperature depression, `T_sky ~ T_air *
  eps_sky^(1/4)`, which is why clear nights cool and humid overcast nights do
  not.

### Layer 2 — Inverse problem and uncertainty (math)

Given an indoor temperature series and weather, recover the model parameters.

- **Identifiability.** From free-running indoor temperature alone, the time
  constant `tau = RC` is identifiable but `R` and `C` are *not separately*
  identifiable without a known heat input. This is provable and worth proving
  explicitly — it is the kind of result that determines what the method can
  honestly claim.
- **Bayesian parameter estimation** with priors drawn from ResStock's published
  housing characteristic distributions. Posterior over `tau` per dwelling
  archetype.
- **Global sensitivity analysis** (Sobol indices) over the parameter space: which
  building property actually controls peak and overnight indoor temperature? The
  answer is directly actionable — it says which retrofit matters.
- **Frequency-domain cross-check.** Fit `tau` in the time domain, then
  independently measure the amplitude damping and phase lag between outdoor and
  indoor temperature and check they agree with `1/sqrt(1+(omega*tau)^2)` and
  `arctan(omega*tau)`. Validating a time-domain fit against an independent
  frequency-domain observable is the strongest internal consistency check
  available here, and it costs almost nothing to run.

### Layer 3 — Attributable and avertable mortality (impact)

Established, citable machinery — not invented here.

- Published exposure-response functions from the distributed-lag non-linear model
  literature (Gasparrini; R package `dlnm` on CRAN), giving relative risk as a
  function of temperature with a minimum-mortality temperature.
- Attributable-death calculation follows Gasparrini & Leone (2014),
  *Attributable risk from distributed lag models*.
- **Comparison:** attributable deaths computed with outdoor exposure vs. with
  modeled indoor exposure, over the same population and period.
- **Counterfactuals:** perturb the physics model to represent an intervention —
  a bedroom window AC unit, night ventilation, a cool roof, added attic
  insulation, or relocation to a cooling centre for six hours — re-run, and
  difference the attributable deaths.
- **Monte Carlo** over model and epidemiological parameter uncertainty to produce
  credible intervals rather than point estimates.

## 4. Staging (each stage is a standalone result)

**Stage 1 — the model works.**
Implement 1R1C and 3R2C. Verify the solver against the closed-form sinusoidal
response. Pull ResStock hourly indoor temperature plus housing characteristics
for Connecticut dwellings. Fit and report accuracy.
*Deliverable:* "A two-parameter lumped model reproduces full EnergyPlus indoor
air temperature across N Connecticut dwellings with RMSE of X °C, and the
recovered time constants agree with known envelope properties."

**Stage 2 — the exposure gap is real and structured.**
Across the stock, during real heat events, quantify how far indoor exposure
departs from the outdoor record — stratified by vintage, AC presence, and
dwelling type.
*Deliverable:* "Outdoor temperature understates night-time heat exposure in
pre-1940 dwellings without air conditioning by X °C, and the error is
systematically largest in the most socially vulnerable housing." This alone
addresses the gap the literature names.

**Stage 3 — the number.**
Apply the exposure-response function under both exposure definitions; run the
intervention counterfactuals.
*Deliverable:* "Substituting modeled indoor exposure for outdoor changes
estimated heat-attributable deaths by X%. Targeting [intervention] at the top
decile of modeled overheating averts Y deaths per 10,000 dwellings per summer
(95% CI a–b), at a cost of $Z per death averted."

If time runs out at any stage, the preceding stages still stand on their own.

## 7. Honest limitations, to be stated in the write-up

1. **This estimates, it does not prove.** The output is a modeled,
   uncertainty-quantified estimate of avertable deaths under stated assumptions.
   It is not a measured mortality reduction, and must never be described as one.
2. **The exposure-response transfer is the sharpest weakness.** Published RR
   curves were estimated *with outdoor temperature as the exposure*. Substituting
   indoor temperature into them without care is not valid. This must be handled
   explicitly — by re-anchoring through the fitted indoor-outdoor mapping, by
   using indoor-specific estimates where they exist (a case-crossover study of
   indoor heat exposure among elderly residents in Houston provides one), and by
   presenting the naive substitution only as a bounded sensitivity analysis.
   Confronting this openly is the difference between a serious result and a
   plausible-looking one.
3. **Validation is against simulation, not measurement.** ResStock is itself a
   model. Agreement with EnergyPlus demonstrates that the reduced-order model
   captures the high-fidelity model's behaviour; it does not independently
   establish that either matches a real building. Measured validation is future
   work, and ecobee is the stated route.
4. **Occupant behaviour is weakly constrained.** Window opening, fan use, and
   shading are behavioural and only crudely represented. This is a known limit of
   the entire field, not of this implementation, but it bounds the claims.

## 8. Success criteria

- The numerical solver reproduces the closed-form sinusoidal frequency response
  to within tight tolerance. Non-negotiable: if this fails nothing downstream is
  trustworthy.
- Recovered time constants correlate with ResStock's known envelope parameters,
  with the strength of that correlation reported honestly.
- The identifiability result for `tau` vs. separate `R` and `C` is demonstrated,
  not assumed.
- Every reported number carries an uncertainty interval and a stated set of
  assumptions.
- A reader can regenerate every figure from public data and the repository.
